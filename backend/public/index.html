For the Kingdom

Vision 2030's Health Sector Transformation Program asks for care that is easier to reach, higher in quality and more efficient, with digital health at its core. Operating rooms are where a hospital's most expensive hours are spent.

  • Access: more patients operated on the day they were promised.
  • Efficiency: fewer overtime riyals for the same number of surgeries.
  • Digital health: an optimisation layer built in the Kingdom, by Saudi-trained engineers.
  • Privacy: designed around the Personal Data Protection Law from the first line.

For your hospital

Four goals we hold ourselves to, each measured in the prototype.

  • The surgery list gets done. A case moves to the next day only when it truly cannot fit.
  • Staff go home on time. Overtime is priced into every plan at your 1.5× policy rate.
  • Emergencies do not break the day. A feasible new schedule in seconds, not phone calls.
  • Nobody is in two places. Every plan is checked for room and surgeon clashes before anyone sees it.

Safety comes before speed

A person decides

ORbit proposes. The OR coordinator confirms and publishes. Nothing reaches staff without a human approval.

No double-booking

A case starts only when its room and its surgeon are both free. The clash count is recomputed and shown with every plan.

No patient identity

The planner reads case ID, date, department, surgeon, room, start and duration. Names and file numbers never enter it.

Every change traced

Each draft, move, emergency and publish is written to an audit log with who did it and when.

    What is not met yet, and why

    • Shift balance (Spec 1). The gap falls from 81% to 49%, not to 15%. The evening shift has fewer surgeons per department; reaching 15% would mean sending patients home. We chose patients. The fix is in the roster, not the model.

    Questions hospitals ask us

    Is ORbit running in a hospital today?

    No. ORbit is a working prototype built as a KFUPM senior design project. It has been tested on synthetic data shaped like a 6-room Saudi hospital. Our next step is a pilot with a mid-size hospital.

    Does it replace our HIS?

    No. Your HIS stays the record of bookings. ORbit takes the day's booked cases, proposes the best arrangement, and hands the confirmed plan back.

    Do you need patient data?

    No. The planner needs seven scheduling columns: case ID, date, department, surgeon, room, booked start and booked minutes. No names, no file numbers, no diagnoses.

    How does it decide where each surgery goes?

    A mixed-integer optimisation model weighs three things: not sending patients home, keeping overtime low, and sharing work fairly between rooms and shifts. Sending a patient home is priced higher than any amount of overtime a room may plan, so it is a last resort.

    What happens when an emergency arrives?

    The coordinator enters the time, department, surgeon and expected minutes. Cases already under way are frozen. The emergency takes the room that frees up first, and the remaining cases are re-timed so no room or surgeon overlaps. In the prototype this takes well under a second.

    Who has the final say?

    Always the OR coordinator. ORbit drafts; a person confirms and publishes. Cases can be pinned so the planner will not move them.

    What does it not do yet?

    It does not reach our 15% shift-balance target or our room-hours target; both are shown as not met in Prototype check, with the reason. It is not yet connected to a live HIS or to NPHIES, and the interface is in English only.

    How much does it cost?

    Signing up is free and gives you 10 actions. After that, our proposed price is SAR 19,000 per operating room per year. For a 6-room hospital that is SAR 114,000 a year.

    Four KFUPM students from Industrial and Systems Engineering, Computer Science and Accounting and Finance, coached by KFUPM faculty. See Who we are.

    ORbit began in 2026 as senior design project M042 at King Fahd University of Petroleum and Minerals in Dhahran, under the project name SORRS. Three departments, one problem: the operating-room schedule is the most valuable plan a hospital makes each day, and it is still built by hand.

    Why mid-size hospitals

    The largest medical cities can buy enterprise platforms and staff analytics teams. A 6-room private or cluster hospital has the same overtime bills and the same emergencies, with one coordinator and a spreadsheet. That hospital is who we build for.

    What we believe

    A patient who was promised a surgery date should get it. A nurse should know when the shift ends. And a tool that plans surgery should show its evidence, including what it has not yet achieved.
